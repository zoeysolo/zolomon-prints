# what bouquet scanography is

Scanography is the practice of making a picture with a flatbed scanner instead of a camera. For a bouquet, that means the flowers are laid directly on the scanner glass and captured at very high resolution, with the light passing across them rather than falling on them from a lens. The result is an image with a kind of depth and detail that a photograph does not usually reach: the translucency of a petal, the fine texture at the edge of a leaf, the exact color of a bloom on the day it was cut.

At Zolomon Prints, each piece starts from a real arrangement, most often a wedding bouquet. Zoey Solomon arranges the flowers on the scanner by hand, scans them at 1200 DPI, and composes the final image before it is printed on archival matte fine art paper. The flowers are photographed, not altered or destroyed in the process.

## why the flatbed matters

A camera sees a bouquet all at once, from one point, under whatever light is in the room. A scanner reads it line by line, in even light, from directly above, at a resolution far higher than most cameras produce. Because the flowers rest on the glass, the focus is consistent from edge to edge and the background falls away to pure black or white. What you get is closer to a botanical study than a snapshot: every stamen and vein is legible, and the color is true to the flower rather than to the lighting.

## how it differs from pressing and resin

Pressed flower art flattens and dries the flowers, then mounts them behind glass. It is a real craft, but it changes the flowers permanently: they lose their dimension and, over time, much of their color.

Resin work seals flowers inside a solid block or coaster. The flowers are preserved as objects, but they are enclosed, and the piece is limited to whatever shape the resin was poured into.

Scanography keeps the flowers as an image. The original blooms are not crushed, dried, or encased. The arrangement is captured at full resolution and then lives as an archival print you can size, frame, and hang like any other piece of art. If you want the flowers themselves to become a picture rather than an object, this is the difference.

## what you can do with it

A scanned bouquet can be printed small enough for a mantel or large enough to anchor a wall. Because the source file is so detailed, the same scan holds up from a garden card to a large-format print. Wedding bouquets are the most common commission, but the same process works for memorial arrangements, garden cuttings, and any flowers worth keeping.
